More and more Slovenian companies use artificial intelligence. According to the Statistical Office of the Republic of Slovenia, 38% of enterprises with at least 10 persons employed used AI in 2026, up from 22% a year earlier. The gap between large and small firms remains wide: 83% of large enterprises use AI, compared with 34% of small ones. Small businesses have not missed the train, but they need to choose carefully where to start.

## The short answer

AI helps most with text-heavy tasks and repeating patterns: drafting emails and quotes, summarising documents, cleaning up data and answering common questions. It helps less with decisions that need context and with work where a mistake is costly. Start with two or three tasks, set rules for use and measure the result after four weeks.

## Where AI really saves time

1. **Drafts of emails, quotes and notices.** AI produces a first version in seconds; you read and adjust it. The saving is biggest for messages you write often and that look alike.
2. **Summaries of long documents.** Tenders, contracts, supplier manuals. AI highlights the key points and deadlines, and the decision stays with you. Always check important details in the original.
3. **Cleaning up data.** Standardising customer or product lists, fixing address formats, sorting into categories. Work that is tedious and error-prone by hand.
4. **Notes from meetings and voice messages.** A transcript and summary with agreed tasks, so nobody forgets who is doing what.
5. **Translation and adapting text.** A quote for a foreign customer, a product description in English or Croatian. Have someone who knows the language check it before it goes out.
6. **Internal guides and FAQs.** Turn existing documents into short guides for new employees or answers to questions that keep coming up.

## Where AI is not (yet) a good choice

- **Calculations without checking.** Language models can state a wrong number with confidence.
- **Legal and tax advice.** AI can help you understand a text, but a professional should confirm the decision.
- **Decisions about people.** Selecting candidates or assessing employees needs human judgement and is specifically regulated.
- **Personal data in free tools.** If you do not know where data is processed and whether it is used to train models, do not put it there.

## What the law requires

Article 4 of the EU AI Act requires companies that use AI to take measures to ensure AI literacy among their staff. The Digital Omnibus on AI, in force since 27 July 2026, softened the obligation: companies no longer have to ensure that every individual reaches a “sufficient level” of knowledge, but measures to build that knowledge remain mandatory.

In practice, for a small business this means:

- short training for everyone who uses AI at work,
- written rules: which tools are allowed, which data must not be entered, who checks the output,
- a simple record of who was trained and when.

GDPR also continues to apply. If you put personal data of customers or employees into an AI tool, you need a proper legal basis and an agreement with the tool provider.

## How to start in four steps

1. **Choose two or three tasks** from the list above that you do every week.
2. **Set rules for use** before you begin. One page is enough.
3. **Run a four-week trial** and measure: how long the task took before and now, and how many corrections were needed.
4. **Train the team** on your own cases. Generic courses rarely change everyday work.

## A sample of short usage rules

> We do not enter customers’ personal data, passwords or trade secrets into AI tools. We only use tools on the approved list. Every text sent to a customer is read by a person first. We always check numbers and deadlines in the original document.

AI does not replace knowledge in a small business. It does save time on work that does not need that knowledge, and you can spend that time with your customers.
